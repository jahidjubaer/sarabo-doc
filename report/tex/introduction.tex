\section{Project Overview}
Sarabo is a web-based electronics repair service management platform developed as an individual final year project. It coordinates the complete life of a repair request (from the moment a customer describes a faulty device to the moment the technician is paid) through one traceable workflow. The platform connects three actors: a \emph{Customer} who needs a repair, a \emph{Technician} who performs it, and an \emph{Admin} who keeps the service running.

The central design decision is that a repair request is a \emph{stateful service case}, not a simple database record. Every important activity (request creation, technician offers, booking, pickup, inspection, quotation, payment, repair, handover, settlement and feedback) moves that case from one well-defined state to another, and the server checks that each move is allowed for that user at that moment. This is what separates Sarabo from a basic create-read-update-delete (CRUD) application: the correctness of a later action depends on earlier actions having happened, and having happened only once.

The project went through two major stages. The first stage (July--August 2026) built the core repair lifecycle with admin-driven technician assignment, and was described in an earlier version of this report. The second stage (September--October 2026) turned Sarabo into a two-sided marketplace: an open jobs board where technicians send offers, a paid inspection fee with automatic refunds, pickup scheduling, customer reviews, email notifications, operational reports, four AI-assisted features and a production deployment. This report describes the system as it stands after both stages.

\section{Problem Statement}
A repair service involves both technical and administrative coordination. A customer needs to describe the device and problem, understand the likely cost before handing the device over, choose someone trustworthy, pay securely and know whether the device has been repaired. A technician needs a steady source of nearby jobs that match their skills, a structured way to record an inspection and quote, and a reliable way to be paid. An administrator needs to see which requests are stuck, which payments need attention, and whether demand is being turned away.

When these activities are handled through phone calls, messaging apps and paper records, inconsistent states easily occur: a customer is charged before agreeing to a price, a technician is booked twice for the same time, an inspection fee is neither refunded nor credited, a technician's earning is paid twice, or private photos of a customer's device are exposed. Sarabo addresses this by modelling the repair process as a role-aware workflow with explicit transition rules, server-side validation and transactional updates.

\section{Motivation}
The project was motivated by five goals:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item to address a real, local problem: making electronics repair in Bangladesh more transparent and trustworthy for customers and more accessible to independent technicians;
  \item to build a realistic full-stack system that goes beyond simple CRUD operations, where roles and state transitions are enforced on the server rather than trusted to the browser;
  \item to integrate production-grade external services (identity, card payment and refunds, private object storage, email, generative AI) without exposing privileged credentials;
  \item to apply generative AI where it genuinely helps users, while keeping it safe: no invented prices, no leaked personal data, and hard usage limits; and
  \item to give every user a clear interface in which the next meaningful action is always visible.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Scope}
The implemented scope includes registration and email verification, technician applications with structured expertise, a versioned service catalogue, AI-assisted request creation, damage-photo upload, pickup scheduling, an open jobs board with offers, inspection-fee booking and refunds, inspection, quotation and revision, Stripe payment, repair progress and completion evidence, receipt confirmation, reviews and private reports, in-app and email notifications, public tracking, technician wallet and bKash withdrawals, an AI customer-support chat, admin reports with an AI summary, and production deployment.

The project does not try to implement every capability of a commercial field-service company. Automated mobile-wallet payouts, spare-parts inventory, warranty claims, live GPS tracking of technicians, native mobile apps and multi-branch accounting are outside the current scope and are discussed as future work in Chapter~\ref{ch:limitations}.
